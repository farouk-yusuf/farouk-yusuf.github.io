The finance brief

Simulated workplace brief

During a finance meeting, I was asked to analyse expenditure trends and identify potential duplicate payments. To ensure the report was relevant, I first confirmed the reporting period and the required focus: cash payments, budget performance, or outstanding commitments.

Requirements elicitation · clarifying the brief
I considered
Could ‘higher expenditure’ mean more cash paid, an overspend against a budget, or an increase in outstanding commitments?
Why it mattered
Each question needs different records. Payment data shows cash outflow; it does not establish budget performance or committed costs.
I clarified
The report would compare cash payments over equivalent dates and identify exceptions for investigation. Budget and commitment reporting would need additional data.

Building a reliable comparison

Department names changed during a restructure. For example, ‘Adults Social Care Directorate’ and ‘Adult Social Care & Health Directorate’ both needed to report under ‘Adult Social Care & Health’. Comparing the original labels could make spending appear to disappear from one department and emerge in another. I standardised the names as shown in the table below.

I also combined Council Management, Finance, HR, OD & Transformation, and Strategy, Insight & Governance into ‘Corporate services (combined)’ for the year-on-year comparison. The restructure moved budgets between these departments, so separate comparisons could mistake organisational changes for changes in spending.

04

Spending rose 21.3%

The export ended on 23 July 2026. I compared 1 April to 23 July in both years, so a partial July was not compared with a full month. Net payments rose from £558.64m to £677.46m: an increase of £118.82m, or 21.3%.

City Operations and Adult Social Care & Health made the largest positive contributions to the increase. Lower payments in corporate services and City Housing partly offset them. This is a change in cash paid, rather than evidence of an overspend against budget.

Before briefing management

Within City Operations, Birmingham Highways payments increased from £20.63m to £87.58m. A £12m credit also required reconciliation. I would ask City Operations to confirm the contract payment schedule and credit before treating the increase as a continuing cost pressure.

Refining the duplicate-payment check

My initial SQL check matched the same supplier and the same payment amount of at least £1,000, within three days. It returned 6,553 payment pairs. Repeated care-package amounts could create legitimate matches, so I refined the screening rule in Python before preparing the AP review list.

  1. Same supplierMatch the supplier identifier, rather than relying on a similar name.
  2. Same amount, at least £1,000Keep the check focused on exact-value payment matches above the screening threshold.
  3. That supplier–amount combination appears exactly twiceExclude combinations repeated throughout the dataset, which can reflect recurring care-package payments.
  4. No more than three days apartRetain closely timed pairs; identify same-day matches for the first review pass.

The refined rule identified 281 candidate pairs worth £4.45m, including 73 paid on the same day. I would start the invoice review with those 73 pairs. The amount represents one potentially repeated payment per pair, rather than the sum of both payments or a confirmed loss.

What this screening rule can miss

This refinement makes the review list more selective. It can still miss duplicates among recurring amounts, below £1,000 or more than three days apart. It can also flag legitimate transactions. Invoice references and AP investigation are needed to confirm the outcome.

Stage 2 · Python refinement

The additional supplier–amount frequency condition is implemented below. It retains combinations appearing exactly twice across the dataset, then checks the gap between those two dates.

x = df[~df.is_personal_data & (df.amount >= 1000)]
two = x[x.groupby(["supplier_number", "amount"]).amount.transform("size") == 2]
pairs = two.sort_values("date").groupby(["supplier_number", "amount"]).agg(
    supplier=("supplier_name_std", "first"), directorate=("directorate", "first"), category=("spend_category", "first"),
    first_date=("date", "min"), second_date=("date", "max"),
    service_area_1=("summary", "first"), service_area_2=("summary", "last")).reset_index()
pairs["days_apart"] = (pairs.second_date - pairs.first_date).dt.days
dups = pairs[pairs.days_apart <= 3].sort_values("amount", ascending=False)
dups["first_date"], dups["second_date"] = dups.first_date.dt.date, dups.second_date.dt.date
K.update(dup_pairs=len(dups), dup_value_m=m(dups.amount.sum()), dup_same_day=int((dups.days_apart == 0).sum()))

07

A separate check identified a privacy risk

While checking the publication fields, I found a separate information-governance issue: identifiers on redacted payments also appeared beside names elsewhere in the published data. This linked 213 redacted payment records across 12 identifiers. I report only the aggregate finding here.

I pseudonymised identifiers for personal-data rows and replaced the display names in the reporting dataset. I would refer the publication issue to Information Governance so they could review how records are released together, rather than checking each redacted row in isolation.

Show the evidence · the aggregate publication check

The workbook’s Personal data check sheet records the aggregate publication issue. No supplier identifiers or personal names are displayed on this page. This check concerns the risk of linking records across a publication; it does not establish whether anyone accessed or misused the information.
