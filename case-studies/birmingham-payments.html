Independent public-data case study

£4.45m of payments worth a closer look.

I narrowed a noisy duplicate-payment check into a review list for Accounts Payable, then found a separate risk in how personal payments were published.

The decision this analysis supports.

A finance team needs two different things from a payments report: a credible explanation of spending changes and a manageable list of exceptions to investigate. I used 247,785 payment lines, covering £3.87bn, to build both. This is an independent portfolio exercise using published data, rather than work commissioned by the council.

What a closer look revealed.

A standard duplicate rule returned 6,553 pairs. Many were repeated care-package rates, so treating every match as a problem would waste review time. I added a one-off-amount condition: the same amount of at least £1,000, for the same supplier, within three days, appearing exactly twice across the dataset. That left 281 candidate pairs, including 73 on the same day.

My recommendation

Start with the 73 same-day pairs and check invoice references in the finance system. The £4.45m is an exception-review amount, not a confirmed loss or a saving I delivered. Published payment records alone cannot distinguish duplicate invoices from legitimate payments or publication duplicates.

The spending increase needs a timing check.

Year-to-date payments rose from £559m to £677m, a 21% increase. Birmingham Highways payments were £88m against £21m a year earlier, with a £12m credit requiring reconciliation. I would confirm the PFI schedule with City Operations before interpreting this as a lasting change. Separately, agency spending rose from £34m to £43m over the matched period.

How I built and checked it.

  1. Cleaned and standardised supplier and directorate fields, mapping 18 source directorate names into stable reporting groups.
  2. Built a SQLite star schema with payments as the fact table and separate supplier, date, directorate and category dimensions.
  3. Combined corporate directorates affected by the September 2025 restructure so budget transfers did not appear as genuine changes in spending.
  4. Compared 1 April to 23 July in both years. A partial July should not be compared with a complete month.
  5. Kept personal-payment reporting aggregated and pseudonymised individual supplier identifiers in the analysis outputs.

What I would ask the team to do next.

Accounts Payable

Review the candidate pairs against invoices, beginning with same-day matches; validate and resolve duplicate supplier records.

City Operations

Reconcile the highways payment schedule and credit before briefing on year-to-date pressure.

Information Governance

Review supplier identifiers on redacted individual payments. Twelve identifiers linked to names elsewhere in the published data; this site publishes only the aggregate finding.

Where the evidence stops.

  • Payments show cash outflow by payment date, not commitments, service costs or budget variance.
  • Invoice references are absent. Possible duplicates require confirmation; candidate pairs are not proven overpayments.
  • 35% of FY2025/26 spend uses the generic PO accrual category, limiting category-level interpretation.
  • Supplier-number and personal-name details are deliberately excluded from the website.

What I learned

The useful output was a more selective review rule. A large exception count can look impressive while creating little practical value for the team expected to check it.
