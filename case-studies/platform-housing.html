Platform Housing Group
Independent public-data case study

Average satisfaction hides a relationship gap.

Platform matches its large-landlord peers overall. The breakdown points to weaker communication and listening, alongside repairs figures that need checking.

The decision this analysis supports.

A board needs to know where service improvement should focus, and whether the comparisons are fair. I benchmarked Platform against 17 large housing associations with at least 40,000 homes, then added complaint outcomes from 21 Midlands landlords. The two peer groups answer different questions and are kept separate.

What a closer look revealed.

Overall satisfaction is 64.6%, up about two percentage points and exactly at the large-landlord median. That average conceals shortfalls in communal areas, information, neighbourhood contribution and listening. My priorities combine the gaps with the measures most closely associated with overall satisfaction: listening and acting, keeping tenants informed, and maintaining homes.

My recommendation

Ask management for an improvement plan with named owners and measures for communication and listening. Keep the apparent repairs decline provisional until the underlying recording change is explained. These are recommendations from an independent analysis, rather than actions implemented by Platform.

A repairs decline that needs a denominator check.

Non-emergency repairs completed within target fell from a rounded 92% to 62%. Over the same period, recorded repairs fell from 90,250 to 13,872 despite the same 28-day target. This suggests a comparability problem worth investigating; it does not establish that the entire performance decline is a recording artefact. ASB and complaint volumes also need definition checks.

How I built and checked it.

  1. Selected large housing associations as the main TSM peer group; comparing with smaller landlords would introduce a known size effect.
  2. Compared measure-level results with peer medians and quartiles rather than relying on a single satisfaction score.
  3. Used cross-landlord correlations to inform prioritisation alongside Platform’s own performance gaps.
  4. Extracted Ombudsman tables from 21 PDF reports and checked category totals against the headline findings.
  5. Checked annual changes against the underlying counts and reporting definitions before treating them as service changes.

What I would ask the team to do next.

Resident engagement

Track how tenant feedback leads to decisions, and report back to tenants on what changed.

Performance reporting

Confirm repairs, ASB and complaint definitions before using the annual movements in a board paper.

Repairs leadership

Review the well-maintained-home gap alongside repairs complaints, rather than assuming prompt complaint responses mean the underlying service is resolved.

Where the evidence stops.

  • Cross-landlord correlation describes association; it does not establish what causes satisfaction to improve.
  • Survey method, sample composition and local conditions can affect comparisons, even within a size-based peer group.
  • Ombudsman cases are an escalated subset of complaints, not a measure of every tenant’s experience.
  • Four extracted Ombudsman reports reconcile within one or two findings because a category label could not be read. The detailed decisions note records this limitation.

What I learned

Choosing a relevant comparison group changed the story. Platform was around the large-landlord average, while the measure-level gaps still gave the board specific issues to investigate.
