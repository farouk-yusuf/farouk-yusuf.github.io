The coders disagreed.
That was a finding worth following.

A validation study taught me why a reassuring average can hide a measurement problem, and why revising the question can matter more than changing the model.

Could AI help code research data reliably?

My PhD investigates why skilled Nigerians migrate to the UK. To analyse a corpus of 29,182 cleaned social media posts about leaving Nigeria, I needed ten yes/no coding questions grounded in my theoretical framework. Before scaling up, I tested whether humans and AI interpreted those questions consistently.

The breakdown changed the next step.

Two human coders worked through a 100-post pilot and 400 further posts. ChatGPT and Gemini were tested as proxy coders. Overall agreement was 88.5% for the human–human comparison and 90.8% for AI–human, while overall alpha cleared the 0.70 target. The weaker question-level results meant that the average was insufficient grounds to trust every part of the scheme.

I had to revisit what the questions measured.

Human disagreements concentrated on Q1, Q3, Q7 and Q8. Q3, the spatial-horizon question, had drifted from the theoretical concept it was meant to capture. I corrected it and refined the codebook across versions v1.1 to v3.3.

Q3 also illustrated a measurement trap: 99.7% raw agreement coexisted with alpha of 0.000 because nearly every answer was “no”. A high agreement percentage was therefore a poor standalone guide to reliability.

I built a coding app that presented one question at a time, saved progress and exported CSV files. A finer instance-level scheme was piloted on 40 posts and set aside when it proved unstable.

What I take into analyst work.

I learned to treat disagreement as evidence about the measurement process. Before trusting a KPI or classifier, I want to know whether its definition is clear, whether important groups fail, and whether the headline metric disguises a weak component.
