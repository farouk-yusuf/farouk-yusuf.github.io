Centrepiece Case Study • Automated Text Classification Audit

Overall agreement reached 90.8%, until the question-by-question breakdown exposed where the coding failed.

Before using OpenAI and Google Gemini models to classify 29,182 cleaned social media posts across ten binary questions (Q0–Q9), I ran a two-stage validation benchmark on 500 posts (a 100-post pilot followed by 400 posts) against two human coders.

The headline trap: Judged solely by the overall average, both the human coders (Krippendorff's alpha 0.758) and the language models (alpha 0.807) cleared the 0.70 reliability target. Stopping there would have let bad data through.

What the breakdown showed: Human coders disagreed systematically on four of the ten questions (Q1, Q3, Q7 and Q8), dropping to an alpha of 0.484 on Q7. Model-to-human reliability dropped to 0.445 on Q9. On Q3 (spatial horizon), raw agreement hit 99.7% but alpha was 0.000: almost every post was a "no", so extreme prevalence made chance agreement near total.

Requirement elicitation & redesign: Auditing the disagreements showed Q3 had drifted from the concept it was meant to measure. I rewrote the classification rules across iterations from v1.1 to v3.3, built a custom web app to present one question at a time with saved progress and CSV exports, and set aside a finer 40-post instance-level pilot after testing showed it was unstable.

My Approach • Requirement Elicitation & Data Quality

Tools are the baseline. Here is how I scope, validate and challenge numbers.

Every project in this portfolio starts with a written requirements brief and a decision log before any chart is built.

1. Requirement Elicitation

I start by defining who reads the output and what decision they face—separating Accounts Payable control checks from Information Governance risks in council data, or matching peer group scale before briefing a housing board.

2. Questioning Unusual Swings

When a metric jumps or collapses, I check the denominator and collection rules first. Since March 2026 I have also worked as an AI model evaluator for Outlier, writing prompts designed to expose logic errors in language models.

3. Self-Taught Technical Build

When manual coding slowed my research, I built a web app to run it. To analyse public spending and Ombudsman PDFs, I taught myself SQL star-schema modelling and Python extraction scripts.

4. Clear Communication

On NatCen's English Housing Survey I achieved a 67% household response rate against a 30% programme benchmark. In April 2026 I co-founded uHired, reaching 1,025 users with zero paid marketing, tracked via Google Analytics and Sheets.

BCC Payments
Duplicate Control		 6,553 pairs of identical payments (£1,000+) to the same supplier within 3 days. Adult Social Care providers bill identical package rates for different residents, flooding standard duplicate rules with false positives. Added an "appears exactly twice in two years" rule to isolate one-off amounts paid twice: 281 pairs (£4.45m), including 73 same-day pairs.
BCC Payments
GDPR & Restructure		 Council Management spend "fell 100%", and 7,681 individual payments (£24.5m) had redacted names. A Sept 2025 restructure moved budgets across corporate directorates. Redacted rows still published supplier numbers, and 12 numbers appeared with names elsewhere (213 payments). Mapped 18 source directorate names into stable groups, and one-way pseudonymised individual supplier IDs so spend totals reconcile safely.
Platform Housing
Pre-Board Check		 Non-emergency repairs in target dropped from 92% to 62%; ASB cases rose from 33 to 118 per 1,000 homes. Total non-emergency repairs counted fell 85% (90,250 to 13,872) under the same 28-day target, pointing to a recording change rather than service collapse. Held both figures—plus Stage 1 complaints rising from 43 to 73 per 1,000 homes—out of the headline narrative for internal verification before board sign-off.
Severn Trent
Sensor & Weather Bias		 Headline storm spills fell 41% (62,085 to 36,471) across changing monitor counts (2,288 to 2,452). Broken monitors (<90% uptime) record fewer spills because they miss events, and 2025 Midlands rainfall was 28% lower (717 mm vs 1,000 mm). Restricted trend comparisons to monitors working ≥90% of the year and compared 2025 against 2022 (721 mm rainfall), showing a 23% underlying drop per overflow.
UHB Waiting Times
Schema & Forecast Audit		 First Python run returned a waiting list of 0; time-series models missed the March 2026 turn (6–8% back-test error). In NHS England Part 2 extracts, the Total column is blank and only Total All is populated. Standard models failed when referrals jumped. Summed weekly time bands directly (96,303 patients) and reported that a 'no change' baseline beat the models (2.5% error), framing March 2027 as a scenario.

The question: Across 247,785 payments over £500 (£3.87bn from July 2024 to 23 July 2026), where are the immediate payment control risks and what is driving the 21% year-to-date spend increase (£677m vs £559m from 1 Apr to 23 Jul)?

What matters most: First, the YTD jump is heavily driven by Birmingham Highways PFI timing (£88m paid this year vs £21m last year, partly offset by a £12m credit), while 35% of FY2025/26 spend sits under a generic "PO accrual" label. Second, 260 suppliers appear under multiple accounts (such as "T C Training Ltd" and "T.C. Training Limited"), and 281 one-off payments (£1,000+) were paid twice within three days. Third, 7,681 redacted payments to individuals (£24.5m) still publish supplier numbers, and 12 of those numbers appear with names elsewhere in the file.

What I recommend:

  1. Accounts Payable: Check the 281 possible duplicate pairs against invoice references in the finance system, starting with the 73 same-day pairs, and merge the 260 duplicate supplier records.
  2. City Operations: Confirm the Birmingham Highways PFI payment schedule before reporting the YTD position.
  3. Information Governance: Stop publishing supplier numbers on redacted individual payments (all IDs are pseudonymised in my outputs).

Audience: Housing Association Board Python PDF Extraction • RSH TSM & Ombudsman Data

Platform Housing Group: Overall satisfaction matches the 64.6% large-landlord median and Ombudsman rulings lead the Midlands, but six tenant relationship measures sit in the bottom quartile.

Platform Gap to Large-Landlord Median (Percentage Points, TP01–TP12)

Benchmarked against 17 large housing associations (40,000+ homes).

Why peer group choice matters: Comparing Platform with all Midlands landlords (median 77%) would make it look worse than it is because large landlords score lower across the sector. Against 17 peer landlords with 40,000+ homes, Platform's 64.6% overall satisfaction (+2 pts YoY) is right on the median.

Operational strength vs. relationship gap: Building safety compliance is 100% across all five checks, emergency repairs in target hit 98.2% (top quartile), and Platform has the lowest Housing Ombudsman maladministration rate of 21 Midlands landlords (38% vs a 57% peer median, down from 64% two years ago, though repairs still account for 23 of 43 upheld findings). However, Platform sits in the bottom quartile on six relationship and neighbourhood measures: communal areas (-9.1 pts), keeping tenants informed (-7.3), neighbourhood contribution (-6.0), listening and acting (-4.9), ASB handling (-4.2) and fair treatment (-1.4).

What I recommend to the Board: Focus improvement on listening and acting (-4.9 pts), keeping tenants informed (-7.3 pts) and home well maintained (-2.0 pts, the strongest correlate with overall satisfaction). Before the board paper is finalised, ask management to confirm three shifts that look like recording changes: non-emergency repairs in target falling from 92% to 62% alongside an 85% drop in counted repairs (90,250 to 13,872), ASB cases jumping from 33 to 118 per 1,000 homes, and Stage 1 complaints rising from 43 to 73 per 1,000 homes.

Separating weather from engineering: Comparing 2025 (717 mm rainfall) directly with 2022 (721 mm rainfall) removes the need for a complex weather model and shows that at equal rainfall, spills per active overflow are 23% lower. Storm tanks at treatment works represent just 13% of overflows but generated 47% of all 2025 spill hours, and the Trent basin holds 58 of the 76 high-spilling sites.

Recommended actions: Fix the 27 repeat-failure monitors that worked under 90% in both 2024 and 2025 (out of 87 below threshold in 2025, driven by 29 installation/design issues, 21 sensor faults and 18 comms faults); review the 13 high-spilling overflows (60+ spills) that have no improvement action logged; and confirm 11 new, 18 removed and 2 surging overflows (+50 spills in a dry year) before publication.

Where UHB stands: UHB met its March 2026 milestones, lifting 18-week performance from 50.2% (Mar 2025) to 68.6% (target 65%) and cutting 52+ week waits to 397 (0.44%, target under 1%). By August 2026, 52+ week waits fell further to 175 (0.18%, a tenth of England's 1.57% rate), and UHB still leads all seven large Midlands trusts compared and sits above England (65.2%).

Why performance slipped 2 points since March: Splitting list growth into clock starts (referrals) and clock stops (treatments and discharges) shows clinics and theatres did not slow down—clock stops were flat year-on-year in April to August (104,806 vs 103,997). Instead, referrals rose 15% (133,091 vs 115,481). If current referral demand continues, the list reaches roughly 107,000 by March 2027, threatening the 70% milestone.

Where to focus clinical capacity: Stabilising the list requires about 1,550 extra clock stops a month (+7% activity) or referral triage with commissioners. ENT (57) and Ophthalmology (53) hold 63% of remaining 52-week waiters, while Gynaecology has seen its waiting list grow 38% in a year and 18-week performance fall 11 points to 45.9% (with Oral Surgery also below 50%).

UHB % Waiting Under 18 Weeks (Jul 2025 – Aug 2026) vs. Milestones

Milestones: 65% (Mar 2026, met at 68.6%) and 70% (Mar 2027 target).

Why I present the 107,000 March 2027 figure as a scenario, not a point prediction

I back-tested both a demand-and-capacity model and a simple trend on the last six months of data. Both missed the March 2026 turning point (6–8% error), whereas a 'no change' baseline had a 2.5% error. Hiding a failed back-test is bad practice, so I report the error rates openly and present March 2027 as a continuation scenario to be refreshed monthly.
