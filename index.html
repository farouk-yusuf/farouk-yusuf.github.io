Birmingham City Council

Explaining expenses and flagging payments for review

Situation
Birmingham City Council’s published payment records show spending information, but changes in department names, repeated payment amounts and limited invoice details make it difficult to interpret how the money was spent.
Task
Prepare a spending summary and flag payment exceptions for the finance manager or Accounts Payable team to investigate.
Action
I processed 247,785 payment records, standardised department names and developed a SQL reporting model. I compared spending across equivalent periods and refined the duplicate-payment rules to reduce false positives from legitimate recurring transactions.
Results
I flagged 281 possible duplicate payment pairs worth £4.45m, including 73 same-day pairs. I also identified a privacy risk affecting 213 redacted payments, which could be linked to names elsewhere in the published records.
Implications
Accounts Payable can prioritise the same-day pairs and check invoices before confirming any overpayments. Information Governance should review how supplier identifiers appear alongside redacted payments. The flagged amount represents potential exceptions, not confirmed losses.

Platform Housing Group

Comparing tenant satisfaction and identifying service priorities

Situation
Platform’s overall satisfaction score offers a broad view of tenant experience, but conceals differences between services. Comparisons with smaller landlords can also distort its relative performance.
Task
Benchmark Platform against similar-sized housing associations, identify improvement priorities and check unusual changes before preparing a board report.
Action
I benchmarked satisfaction measures against 17 large housing associations and extracted Ombudsman findings for 21 Midlands landlords. I examined gaps in individual measures, compared complaint outcomes and checked annual movements against the underlying reporting volumes.
Results
Platform’s 64.6% overall satisfaction matched the large-landlord median. However, keeping tenants informed scored 7.3 percentage points below the median, while listening and acting scored 4.9 points below. Recorded repair volumes also fell sharply alongside the reported decline in repairs performance, raising concerns about comparability.
Implications
Management should prioritise communication, listening and home maintenance. The performance team should confirm reporting definitions before presenting the repairs decline as a straightforward change in service quality.

Severn Trent Water

Assessing sewage-spill reductions and prioritising sites for review

Situation
Storm overflows can release sewage into rivers when the sewer system is overwhelmed. Severn Trent reported fewer sewage spills in 2025, but lower rainfall may explain part of the improvement. Gaps in monitoring can also understate spills when sensors fail to record events.
Task
Assess the reported improvement, account for rainfall and monitoring coverage, and identify sites requiring maintenance or investment review.
Action
I combined six years of environmental returns, standardised file formats and checked monitor reliability. I compared spill trends with rainfall, examined years with similar rainfall and identified sites with persistent spills or recurring monitor failures.
Results
Reported spills fell 41%, while regional rainfall fell 28%. Across reliably monitored sites, average spills were approximately 23% lower in 2025 than in 2022, a year with similar rainfall. However, 76 sites recorded at least 60 spills in 2025, including 13 without a reported improvement action.
Implications
Managers should report spill reductions alongside rainfall and monitoring coverage, then prioritise the remaining high-spill sites for review. Similar annual rainfall strengthens the comparison but does not isolate the effects of engineering improvements or local weather conditions.

University Hospitals Birmingham

Explaining waiting-list growth and assessing future pressure

Situation
UHB improved its waiting-time performance by March 2026. By August, its waiting list had grown and fewer pathways met the 18-week standard.
Task
Explain the renewed pressure, identify specialty-level concerns and assess whether forecasting could support recovery planning.
Action
I processed 41 months of NHS reporting, checked completeness and reconciled reporting fields. I compared UHB with six large Midlands trusts and England, examined referrals and completed pathways, and tested two forecasting methods against a no-change baseline.
Results
The waiting list grew 7.5% between March and August 2026. Referrals increased approximately 15% year on year during April–August, while completed pathways remained broadly flat. Both forecasting methods produced larger back-test errors than the simple baseline.
Implications
The performance team should investigate rising referrals alongside available capacity and specialty pressures. Planners should treat the March 2027 estimate as a conditional scenario, refresh its assumptions monthly and retain the baseline comparison.
